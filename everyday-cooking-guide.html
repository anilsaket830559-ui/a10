The Everyday Cooking Guide

Seasoning, heat and timing: the small number of habits that separate food you tolerate from food you look forward to.

Most people who cook badly are not lacking recipes. They are missing four or five habits that nobody ever told them, because cookbooks assume you already have them. This guide is those habits.

Season in layers

Salt added at the start does something different from salt added at the end. Early salt draws water out of onions and mushrooms so they brown; salt in the cooking water seasons pasta and vegetables from the inside; salt at the table sits on the surface and hits the tongue first. If you only salt at the end, the inside of the food stays bland however much you add.

Keep a small bowl of flaky salt within reach and taste at three points: after the aromatics soften, halfway through the simmer, and just before serving.

Get the pan hot and leave things alone

Browning is flavour. It needs three things: a hot, dry surface, enough space, and the patience to not stir. A crowded pan releases steam faster than it can evaporate, and steamed meat is grey and sad. Cook in two batches rather than one if the pan is full.

You can tell the pan is ready when a drop of water skitters across it rather than sitting and hissing.

Acid at the end

Rich food needs something sharp to stop it feeling heavy. A squeeze of lemon, a splash of vinegar, a spoonful of yoghurt or a few capers will lift a dish that tastes flat. Add it off the heat, taste, and add a little more. Acid is the single most under-used tool in home kitchens.

Mise en place is not showing off

Chopping everything before the pan goes on sounds fussy until the first time you burn garlic while dicing an onion. For anything that moves fast, such as a stir fry or a risotto, lay everything out first. For a slow stew you can chop as you go.

Build a meal from what you have

Almost every satisfying plate has the same four parts: something substantial, something green, something sharp and something crunchy. Rice, roasted vegetables, a yoghurt dressing and toasted seeds is dinner. So is pasta, wilted greens, lemon and breadcrumbs. Once you see the pattern you stop needing a recipe for a Tuesday.

Weigh things

A cup of flour can vary by a third depending on how it was scooped, which is why the same recipe behaves differently in different hands. Scales remove that variable for about the price of two takeaways and will improve your baking more than any other purchase.

Timing a whole meal

Work backwards from when you want to eat. Start with the thing that takes longest and can sit, usually the oven dish. Anything green goes last. Resting meat is not an optional flourish: ten minutes under foil keeps the juice in the meat instead of on the board.

When you are cooking for more people than the recipe expects, run it through the Portion Scaler first; the quantities and the pan size change at different rates.
